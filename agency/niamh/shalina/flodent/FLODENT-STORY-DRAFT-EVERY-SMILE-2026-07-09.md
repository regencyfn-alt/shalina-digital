## 1 · THE STORY

It's Show and Tell morning in the Mkhize house, and Buhle — the youngest, the one who inspects everything — has been ready for days. At the kitchen table she finishes colouring the picture that's going to school with her: her whole family, and she has given every one of them a big bright smile. That's the point of the picture. That's her family. She sets it down carefully as Zimkitha calls her for breakfast.

Then the world ends the way it only ends when you're eight. Reaching for her cup of tea, she tips it. Tea runs across the table and across the drawing; the colours bleed into one large brown stain, right through her family. Buhle freezes. Devastated. And nobody swoops in with a speech — that's not how this house works.

Before school, the bathroom. Buhle brushes her teeth with Flodent the way she does everything: thoroughly, seriously, inspecting the job as she goes. And when she looks up at herself in the mirror, the smile that comes back at her is the very thing her drawing was about. Her confidence walks back in — and with it, the Inspector's eye. Because held up to the light, that stain isn't a ruin at all. That stain is shaped exactly like a tree.

Back at the table, tongue out in concentration, she works. The stain becomes a great spreading family tree, and beneath its branches she redraws her family, every face wearing the bright smile — and on a little shelf in the corner of her drawing, tiny and precise because the Inspector misses nothing, the family's Flodent, right where it lives.

At school, Buhle presents her artwork and tells the truth: the mistake became her favourite part of the picture — because every morning starts with a smile. Her classmates applaud. The teacher hangs the drawing on the wall.

And Buhle runs home, schoolbag bouncing, bursting to tell everyone — because the drawing isn't just a picture anymore. It's a story about confidence, creativity, and starting fresh.

**End frame: Flodent. Every day is a fresh start.**

## 2 · WHY THE AUDIENCE WILL LOVE IT

Every parent has watched a small disaster wipe out a child's proud morning, and every adult remembers being that child. The spill is universal; the freeze is universal; the recovery is the story everyone wants told. Buhle wins them twice: first as the devastated eight-year-old, then as the Inspector who out-thinks her own catastrophe — no adult rescues her, and audiences love a child who solves it herself. The stain-becomes-tree turn is the shareable moment: it reframes every mess as a picture waiting to be finished, and viewers will send it to someone who needs that today. The second watch pays too — the tiny Flodent drawn on the shelf inside her artwork is the detail people spot on the classroom wall and point out in the comments.

## 3 · WHY THE BRAND BENEFITS

The brand owns the exact beat it claims: confidence returning at the bathroom sink. The brushing scene is our permissioned space — product in use, natural, never performed — and the story's whole turn happens there, so Flodent is structurally inseparable from the recovery without one claim being spoken. Buhle debuts as the sixth Mkhize on a story that defines her (the Inspector finds what everyone else missed), giving the family universe its child lead and unlocking the youngest demographic mirror: kids copy her seriousness; parents recognise the ritual working. And the drawn tube inside her artwork is immersive placement at its purest — the brand living on a shelf in a child's picture of her own family. That is the campaign thesis in one frame.
